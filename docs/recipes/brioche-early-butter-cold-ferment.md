# Brioche (Stand Mixer, Cold Ferment)

Use the mixer to build a stretchy dough, then add the butter gradually. An overnight chill makes
the rich dough much easier to shape. Expect it to be soft and tacky after mixing and firm when cold.

Use the [Brioche Calculator](https://kitchenratio.com/calculator/bread/brioche/) to scale the
ingredients for your pan and loaf count.

## Formula and Ingredients

The percentages match the calculator's brioche preset. Weigh eggs **without shells**: beat them
together, then weigh the amount needed. Egg count alone is too variable for a scaled batch.

| Ingredient | Baker's % | One 750g test loaf |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bread flour | 100% | 330.4g |
| Whole eggs | 64% | 211.5g |
| Unsalted butter, pliable | 41% | 135.5g |
| Sugar | 11% | 36.3g |
| Salt | 2% | 6.6g |
| Active dry yeast | 2% | 6.6g |
| Whole milk | 7% | 23.1g |

The milk is the yeast-hydration liquid and is **already included** in the formula; do not add
extra milk or water for that step. Small rounding differences are normal.

**For finishing:** one additional egg, beaten, for brushing the loaf. This is separate from the
dough ingredients; you will not need all of it.

The 750g starting point draws on a similar rich dough recommended for a 9 × 5-inch pan in

development, pan taper, and proofing can change the result. Record your pan dimensions, dough
weight, and baked fill before adjusting the next batch.

## Day 1: Mix and Chill

### 1. Prepare the Butter and Yeast

- Cut the butter into about **8 small portions**. It should dent easily when pressed and bend
  without cracking, while still holding its shape. It should not be shiny, oily, or melted.
- Keep the eggs cool to help limit warming during mixing.
- Warm the measured milk to about **100°F (38°C)**, or follow your yeast packet's hydration
  temperature. Stir in the active dry yeast and a pinch of sugar taken from the measured amount.
- Let stand **5–10 minutes**, then stir again to distribute the softened granules. This small
  quantity may form a paste rather than a tall layer of foam.

### 2. Develop the Dough Before Adding Butter

- Put the flour, eggs, remaining sugar, salt, and all the yeast-and-milk mixture in the mixer bowl.
  **Leave the butter out for now.**
- Mix with a black silicone spatula until the flour is moistened. Fit the dough hook and knead at
  the speed recommended for yeast dough by your mixer manufacturer—**speed 2 on a KitchenAid**.
- Stop the mixer to scrape down dry patches or dough collecting above the hook.
- Continue until the rough mixture becomes cohesive and elastic. A small piece should stretch
  several inches before tearing, rather than breaking immediately. It does not need a perfect
  windowpane yet.

Allow roughly **5–10 minutes of kneading as a starting estimate**, but judge the dough.
Mixer size and batch size affect how quickly the hook catches and develops it. Follow the
machine's duty limits and pause if the motor needs a rest.

### 3. Add the Butter Gradually

- Stop the mixer and add one butter portion. Restart at the recommended kneading speed.
- Wait until that portion is worked into the dough and large butter streaks disappear before
  adding the next. Stop to scrape the bowl with the silicone spatula when needed.
- The dough may smear around the bowl or look temporarily broken after an addition. Let it come
  back together; do not correct this by adding flour.
- Once all the butter is in, continue until the dough is smooth, satiny, and stretchy. It may
  still cling to the bottom of the bowl.

Butter incorporation and finishing may take **another 10–20 minutes**, with pauses as needed.
If the dough becomes warm, greasy, or begins leaving liquid butter in the bowl, stop, cover,
and refrigerate the bowl for **15–20 minutes** before continuing.

### 4. Check the Windowpane

Stop the mixer. Pinch off a small piece, let it relax for a minute, then gently stretch it
between your fingertips. The center should become a thin membrane that lets light through
before tearing. If it tears while still thick, knead a little longer and check again.

The finished dough should be very soft and tacky, but hold together when lifted with a scraper.
Chilling firms the butter; it does not replace this dough-development step.

### 5. Start Fermentation, Then Refrigerate

- Scrape the dough into a lightly greased container with room for expansion. Cover.
- Leave at room temperature for about **45–60 minutes**, until it begins to look a little puffy.
  Do not wait for a full doubling before chilling.
- Gently press down any large bubbles, cover securely, and refrigerate **12–24 hours**.

The dough should be thoroughly cold and firm before shaping. Use a broad container rather than
a deep, tightly packed mass so it chills more evenly.

## Day 2: Shape, Proof, and Bake

### 6. Shape the Cold Dough

- Grease the loaf pan. A strip of parchment across the base and long sides can help lift it out.
- Weigh out the dough for each pan while it is still cold. Lightly flour the work surface.
- Gently press one portion into a rectangle, removing large trapped bubbles. Make the short edge
  slightly shorter than the inside length of your pan.
- With that short edge nearest you, roll the dough away from you into a snug log. Press lightly
  as you roll to avoid trapping big air pockets; do not pull hard enough to tear the surface.
- Pinch the final seam closed along the log. Tuck in the ends if needed and place it
  **seam-side down** in the pan.

If the dough resists flattening, cover and rest it **5–10 minutes**, then try again. If it softens
and starts smearing, chill it briefly. You want cool, pliable dough, not a warm sticky mass.

### 7. Proof Until Puffy

Cover with lightly greased wrap or a roomy cover that will not touch the rising dough. Proof
around **70–75°F (21–24°C)**. Allow roughly **2–4 hours**, and longer if the dough or room is cool.
Cold dough needs time to warm before its rise becomes obvious. Avoid a hot proofing spot that
melts the butter.

Look for the loaf to expand noticeably, fill out the pan, and become softly rounded and slightly
jiggly when the pan is nudged. With a lightly floured fingertip, make a shallow indentation at the
side of the dome: it should recover slowly and leave a slight mark. A quick spring-back suggests
more time is needed. A dent that stays completely, with sagging or fragile dough, can indicate
overproofing.

Use those cues together. **Do not wait for a fixed height above the rim**: pan depth and these
provisional dough weights make that unreliable.

### 8. Preheat and Egg Wash

As the loaf approaches readiness, preheat the oven to **350°F (175°C)** with a rack positioned
so the top of the loaf will sit near the center of the oven.

Beat the extra egg until uniform. Brush a thin coat gently over the dough just before baking.
Avoid pressing out the gas or letting egg collect between the dough and pan.

### 9. Bake and Check the Center

Bake for roughly **30–45 minutes** for these 675–750g test loaves. Start checking around 30 minutes;
your pan material and oven may require longer.

Look for a well-browned top and a set loaf. If the top colors before the center is ready, tent it
loosely with foil and continue baking. Insert an instant-read thermometer into the center of the
loaf without touching the pan; aim for **190–195°F (88–91°C)**. Check another central spot if the
reading seems inconsistent. Crust color alone does not tell you whether the center is baked.

### 10. Cool Before Slicing

Rest in the pan for about **10 minutes**, then carefully turn out onto a wire rack. Let the loaf
cool fully before slicing, usually **at least 1–2 hours**. Cutting it hot can compress the crumb
and make the center seem gummy.

Expect a soft, tender crumb that pulls into strands. It should not have a wet or paste-like band
through the middle once cooled.

## Troubleshooting

- **Sticky but elastic:** normal for this rich dough. Use a scraper and the overnight chill.
- **Greasy or butter pooling:** stop mixing and cool the dough before continuing.
- **Tears before stretching thin:** allow a brief rest, then continue kneading and retest.
- **Slow final proof:** give cold dough more time; check puffiness rather than raising the heat sharply.
- **Loaf too low or overflowing:** record pan depth, dough weight, and proofing cues before changing the next batch.

This dough can also be used for cinnamon rolls, but filling, tray loading, and baking time need
their own instructions. The loaf weights and bake times above do not apply directly to a tray of rolls.
